Six hundred years of stars

Stellation has been done by a Venetian mosaicist, an imperial astronomer whose mother was tried as a witch, a Prussian schoolmaster with a room full of paper models, and a Worcester teacher who fell out with Coxeter over prose style. Here is the chronology, with the solids live so you can turn them — and, where it is a fair fight, build them yourself.

Before anyone called it mathematics
c. 1430

A floor in Venice

St Mark's Basilica · attributed to Paolo Uccello

Set into the marble pavement of the Basilica di San Marco is a small panel showing a spiky twelve-pointed solid drawn in perspective. It is a small stellated dodecahedron, and it is roughly two centuries older than the first mathematical description of one.

The attribution to Paolo Uccello is traditional rather than documented, and how firm it is depends on whom you ask. What is not in doubt is that somebody in quattrocento Venice worked out how the thing looked from an angle and rendered it in stone, for a floor most people would walk over without looking down.

✎

Uccello was famously, ruinously obsessed with perspective. Vasari tells a story of him working all night and brushing off his wife's calls to bed with a remark about how sweet perspective was — a line repeated in every art history survey since, and about as reliable as anything else in Vasari.

1509

Leonardo draws the elevated solids

Luca Pacioli, De divina proportione · illustrations by Leonardo da Vinci

Pacioli's treatise on proportion, written in the 1490s and printed in Venice in 1509, carries sixty solids drawn by Leonardo — including "elevated" forms with a pyramid raised on every face. The icosaedron elevatum is very nearly the first stellation of the icosahedron; the difference is the height of the pyramids, and it matters, because a true stellation must put its faces in the original's planes and Leonardo's elevations do not always do that.

Leonardo's drawings are also the first well-known use of the open, skeletal vacuum style — solids drawn as struts, so you can see through them. Whether the idea was his or Pacioli's is unsettled.

The four stars
1611

Kepler, on a snowflake, invents a name

Johannes Kepler, Strena seu de nive sexangula

Kepler's New Year's gift to a patron — a short book on why snowflakes have six corners — contains along the way the stella octangula, the eight-pointed star you get by extending an octahedron's faces until they close again. It is the same figure as two tetrahedra interpenetrating, and its eight points are the corners of a cube.

This is the whole subject in its simplest instance, and the octahedron is unusual in having exactly one stellation. There is nothing to choose. Add the only shell there is:

An unexpected afterlife Kepler's little snowflake book asks why crystals should take on regular shapes at all, and guesses that it has to do with how spheres pack. That guess — the Kepler conjecture — went unproved until Thomas Hales announced a computer-assisted proof in 1998, which took a decade to check and a further formal-verification project, completed in 2014, to settle.
1619

Two stars in the Harmony of the World

Johannes Kepler, Harmonices Mundi, Book II

Eight years later Kepler describes two more: the small stellated dodecahedron and the great stellated dodecahedron. Both come from the dodecahedron, and the dodecahedron is a gift to a tutorial, because it has exactly three stellations — and all three are famous.

The dodecahedron and everything it can become:

Walking outward one shell at a time takes you through three of the four Kepler–Poinsot star polyhedra in order. Kepler had the first and the third. The middle one he missed.

☾

What else Kepler was doing. He finished Harmonices Mundi in May 1618; the book contains his third law of planetary motion, which he worked out in the same weeks. He had also spent much of 1615–1621 defending his mother Katharina, who was accused of witchcraft in Leonberg, imprisoned, and threatened with torture. She was released in 1621 and died the following spring. Kepler wrote parts of his most serene book while conducting her legal defence.

1809

Poinsot finds the other two

Louis Poinsot, Mémoire sur les polygones et les polyèdres

Two centuries after Kepler, Poinsot — a mechanician better remembered for rigid body rotation — described the great dodecahedron and the great icosahedron, apparently without knowing Kepler had been there for the other two. The great icosahedron is the only one of the four that is a stellation of the icosahedron rather than the dodecahedron. Its twelve spikes reach φ³ ≈ 4.236 times the core's circumradius.

Seven shells of the icosahedron's eight. Try the row numbers, one at a time.

In 1813 Cauchy proved that Kepler's two and Poinsot's two are the whole list: there are exactly four regular star polyhedra and no more.

The enumerators
1876

Hess, and the compounds

Edmund Hess

Hess described the interpenetrating compounds — five octahedra, five tetrahedra, ten tetrahedra — that turn out to be stellations of the icosahedron. Some sources argue Cauchy already had them in 1812; the credit is not settled, and the page that tells you otherwise is oversimplifying.

The compound of five octahedra is a good one to build, because it is a trap. Its thirty outer points sit exactly where an octahedron's would, so it is tempting to take the shell that produces them and stop. That gives the right silhouette and a hollow interior. The real compound needs the shell underneath as well.

1900

Brückner's room full of paper

Max Brückner, Vielecke und Vielflache: Theorie und Geschichte (Teubner, Leipzig)

Max Brückner (1860–1934) was a German schoolmaster who built polyhedra out of card. Not a few — hundreds. His 1900 book, Polygons and Polyhedra: Theory and History, is the great survey of the subject at the turn of the century: several hundred line engravings, and behind them ten plates of photographs recording a collection of 146 paper models he had made himself.

The photographs are the thing. Rows of small white solids arranged on shelves like specimens, each casting its own shadow — an amateur's cabinet of curiosities that happened to be the most complete catalogue of star polyhedra in existence. Brückner pushed stellation past the four regular stars into the irregular forms nobody had systematically listed. How much of it was new with him is still argued: one tally credits him with ten stellations of the icosahedron including the outermost one; another counts six as genuinely his.

The outermost stellation of the icosahedron — among the figures Brückner modelled and photographed. Later named the echidnahedron, after the spiny anteater, in 1995.

✦

Why this book matters more than its citations suggest. Brückner's plates are where the next generation got their picture of the subject. When Coxeter's group came to enumerate the icosahedron's stellations in the 1930s, Brückner is the state of the art they were extending. His book is also the reason a great many people who never read a word of German know what these solids look like: you can turn the pages and simply see them.

The whole thing has been scanned and is free to read. The models themselves are a harder question — a schoolmaster's paper collection is not the sort of thing that survives a century by default.

1924

A schoolteacher in Worcester

Albert Harry Wheeler (1873–1950), International Congress of Mathematicians, Toronto

Wheeler taught school in Worcester, Massachusetts, and made models — hundreds of them, like Brückner. In 1924 he stood up at the ICM in Toronto and described something new: a method. Mark out regions on the stellation diagram, take the cells that sit behind them, and you generate figures systematically instead of finding them one at a time. He listed twenty forms, twenty-two counting reflections, the outermost one among them.

That method is, in essence, what the program on this site does.

✂

The falling-out. Wheeler was to have been a co-author of The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra. He withdrew, objecting to how Coxeter wrote — complaining, in a line that has survived him, that Coxeter had a way of taking a subject and tying it in knots. Coxeter took his name off the title page and the book came out without him.

There is a second, quieter consequence. Wheeler chose to describe only the visible regions of a face, and Miller's rules inherited the habit — so the 1938 enumeration looks at outer surfaces and ignores what is going on inside. Later critics think that decision cost the subject some solids.

1938

The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra

H. S. M. Coxeter, P. Du Val, H. T. Flather, J. F. Petrie · University of Toronto Press

Twenty-six pages and twenty plates, and the definitive statement of the problem. The division of labour is worth knowing, because each part of it survives in the program:

  • Coxeter did the enumeration, running it on a graph of which region-sets adjoin which — the direct ancestor of the support relation that shift-click uses.
  • Du Val invented the cell notation: shells a–h, subscripts for the different shapes within a shell, roman versus italic for the two hands of a chiral one. That is the Cells table, three columns and all.
  • Flather made the models — all fifty-nine, in card.
  • Petrie drew the plates.
  • J. C. P. Miller supplied the five rules that fix the number at 59, and was not an author at all.
△

Flather had started early. He was making these models before he met Coxeter in 1932, including ones that Miller's rules would later disqualify. The models went to Cambridge, and photographs of them appear in the third edition.

Petrie was the son of the Egyptologist Flinders Petrie, and had a reputation for being able to see in four dimensions. Petrie polygons are named after him.

Du Val went on to a second kind of fame entirely: the Du Val singularities of algebraic geometry — the ADE surface singularities — carry his name. The man who invented the notation for shells of cells around an icosahedron also named a fundamental object in a field with no obvious connection to it.

The book has been reissued twice: a Springer reprint in 1982, and a third edition from Tarquin in 1999, reset and redrawn by Kate and David Crennell, who added the 1–59 index numbering everyone now uses.

Du Val's Ef₁, one hand of it: the compound of five tetrahedra. One of only two chiral stellations that are fully supported, which is why it is such a popular paper model.

Afterlives
1948–1961

Escher, working from the plates

M. C. Escher

Escher put star polyhedra into his prints repeatedly: Stars (1948), with chameleons inside a hollow frame; Gravitation (1952), where lizards live in a small stellated dodecahedron; Order and Chaos (1950). He was not inventing these shapes — he was working from the literature, and Brückner's plates are among the things he had to hand.

The first stellation of the rhombic dodecahedron — the solid at the top of Escher's Waterfall (1961), and now generally called Escher's solid.

Mathematician meets artist Coxeter and Escher corresponded. Coxeter sent him a diagram of a hyperbolic tessellation; Escher, who could not follow the mathematics, took the picture and produced the Circle Limit series from it. Escher said more than once that he did not understand the theory — he just saw what the figure was doing.
1971

A monk with a knife and a lot of card

Magnus Wenninger, Polyhedron Models

Wenninger was a Benedictine monk who taught mathematics in the Bahamas and built polyhedral models for decades. Polyhedron Models gave the nets and the instructions, and put these objects within reach of anyone with card and patience. The per-shell cell counts for the icosahedron that this program computes — 20, 30, 60, 20, 60, 120, 12, 30, 60, 60 — are the numbers tabulated in his book.

1982

Five-fold symmetry turns up in metal

Dan Shechtman

Crystallography had a theorem: a crystal cannot have five-fold symmetry, because pentagons do not tile the plane and icosahedra do not fill space. On 8 April 1982 Shechtman recorded a diffraction pattern from an aluminium–manganese alloy with unmistakable ten-fold symmetry, wrote three question marks in his notebook, and spent the next two years being told he was wrong.

The cost of being right Shechtman was asked to leave his research group. Linus Pauling — twice a Nobel laureate — dismissed the work publicly and repeatedly. The paper finally appeared in 1984. Shechtman received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011, alone. The International Union of Crystallography had by then rewritten the definition of a crystal to accommodate what he found.
1962 · 1985

Icosahedra that build themselves

Caspar and Klug · Kroto, Curl and Smalley

Donald Caspar and Aaron Klug worked out in 1962 why so many viruses are icosahedral: it is the most economical way to build a closed shell from many copies of one protein. Some capsids are visibly spiky — stellated, in effect — because the protein subunits stand proud at the vertices.

In 1985 Kroto, Curl and Smalley found C₆₀: sixty carbon atoms at the vertices of a truncated icosahedron, the shape of a football. They named it buckminsterfullerene after the architect of the geodesic dome, and shared the 1996 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it.

1998–

The program you are using

Vladimir Bulatov

The Stellation applet was written between 1998 and 2001 — a Java applet, which is to say software for a web that no longer exists. Its Cells window is Du Val's notation made clickable, and as far as a survey of the field can tell, no other stellation program has offered that particular table.

This site is a JavaScript port of it, checked against the original: the same facet counts, the same cell structure, the same volumes to six decimal places. The original Java still compiles and runs, too, which after twenty-five years is its own small monument.
