Cells walkthrough

The Cells table is the control that actually builds a stellation. It looks like a wall of numbers. It is really a map of the shells of space around a polyhedron — and once you can read one row of it, you can build the great icosahedron by hand in about four clicks.

Every figure below is live and clickable. They run the same code as the app.

1 · The idea, in two dimensions

Take a pentagon and extend its five sides. Each side runs on until it meets another, and the crossings outline a five-pointed star — a pentagram. Nothing was added: it is the same five lines, followed further.

That is a stellation. The word is Kepler's, from stella, star; he used the construction in 1619 to obtain two star polyhedra that now carry his name.

In three dimensions you extend a polyhedron's face planes rather than a polygon's edges. The planes carry on past the solid, cut through one another, and slice the space around it into bounded pieces.

2 · Twenty planes, seen from one face

An icosahedron has twenty faces, so twenty planes. Stand on one of them and look straight down: the other nineteen cross your plane in a set of lines, and those lines cut it into regions. This picture is the stellation diagram, and it has been the working drawing of the subject since Max Brückner's Vielecke und Vielflache of 1900.

The little triangle at dead centre is the icosahedron's own face. Everything further out is space the extended planes have carved up. The ten-pointed star is characteristic of the icosahedron; every solid has its own signature diagram.

A distinction worth getting right early. The regions you see here are two-dimensional: they are pieces of one plane. The things you select to build a solid are three-dimensional cells — the little volumes the full set of planes encloses. A cell is bounded above and below by regions like these, generally on different planes. The diagram is a cross-section of the structure, not the structure itself.

3 · Cells, and the shells they sit in

The twenty planes cut the space around the icosahedron into 67 bounded cells. They are not scattered at random: they fall into concentric shells, and the program numbers those shells layers, counting outward from the solid itself.

Layer 0 is the icosahedron. Layer 1 is the first shell of cells resting on its faces — twenty of them, one per face. Layer 2 is the next shell out, and so on to layer 7, where the last cells run off into the long spikes of the diagram.

These layers are not an invention of the program. Patrick Du Val, working on The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra in the 1930s, noticed exactly this structure and named the shells with letters — a for the core, then b through h. Eight shells, which is what the table has. He counted them by power: how many face planes a ray from the centre crosses on its way out to the cell. That is the same number the program computes for every facet, and the reason the rows come out in this order.

4 · How to read one row

Here is the whole grammar of the table. A row is a layer. Along it sit the cells of that layer — but not one box per cell, which would be unusable: layer 3 alone has sixty cells.

Instead, cells that the solid's symmetry can carry onto one another are bundled into a single box. Take one of layer 1's twenty cells and spin the icosahedron: it lands on each of the other nineteen in turn. They are the same cell in twenty places, so they get one box. Mathematically the bundle is an orbit of the symmetry group; practically it is the smallest thing you can add without breaking the symmetry.

What the coloured bars mean

The bar under a box is not decoration and it is not a selection state. It says how many congruent cells the box stands for — one hue per count. For the icosahedron only six counts occur in the whole table:

So every box with the same colour holds the same number of pieces, and that number is not arbitrary: it is 120 divided by how much symmetry a single cell keeps, as the table below shows. Scanning a column of colour down the table is scanning for cells of the same kind — all the vertex-centred ones, all the edge-centred ones — which is precisely the structure you need to see when you are hunting for a particular solid.

The one to watch for is the rarest: , which occurs exactly once, in layer 5. That is the bundle with no symmetry of its own, and the only one that splits into two hands.

That splitting is not decoration. The program groups cells under the full symmetry of the polyhedron (for the icosahedron, the group Ih, 120 operations including reflections) and then splits each bundle under the symmetry you want the stellation to have (I, the 60 rotations alone). A bundle that is unchanged by reflection stays whole. A bundle that is not — a chiral one — falls into two mirror halves, and you may take just one.

Why the counts are what they are

Every number in the table is forced. A bundle's size is the order of the symmetry group divided by how much symmetry a single cell has left — the orbit–stabiliser theorem, doing all the work. Since Ih has 120 operations:

Add them up: 472 cells outside the core, which is the count in the literature. And notice the one anomaly — the 120-cell bundle in layer 5 is the only one whose cells keep no symmetry at all. That is exactly why it is the only bundle that splits. A cell with a mirror in its own stabiliser is its own reflection; a cell with none has a distinct mirror twin somewhere else in the bundle, and the bundle falls in half.

Du Val wrote the same thing with typography: where a shell held more than one shape he added subscripts (e1, e2, f1…), and where a shape came in two hands he set the right-handed one in roman and the left-handed one in italic. Layer, bundle, sub-cell. The table you are clicking is his notation with the italics turned into checkboxes.

5 · Cells have to rest on something

Select a cell from layer 5 on its own and you get twelve spikes hanging in mid-air, touching nothing. It is a legitimate set of cells and a perfectly useless solid.

Compare:

The second selection is the first plus every cell beneath it, all the way down to the core. In the app you get it in one gesture: shift-click the cell and the program follows the chain of contacts downward and takes the whole supporting set.

The third button is the trap. {0,2} — core plus shell c, skipping shell b — looks exactly like the compound of five octahedra from outside, because the missing shell is buried. It is hollow. The real compound is {0,1,2}, and the only way to tell them apart is to ask whether every cell is resting on something. This is the sort of mistake the support relation exists to prevent, and it is easy to make by eye.

Miller's rules, and where 59 comes from. When Coxeter, Du Val, Flather and Petrie published The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra in 1938, the number 59 was not a discovery about space — it was a consequence of a definition. The criteria they adopted came from J. C. P. Miller: faces must lie in the twenty planes, the figure must have the icosahedron's symmetry, each face must be a single connected piece, and figures that merely touch along edges or that can be got more simply are excluded. Change the rules and you change the number.

One clause matters here more than the rest. Miller's third rule asks the parts in each face plane to have three-fold symmetry with or without reflection — and that "with or without" is precisely the Ih-versus-I dial in the program's symmetry panel. Of the 59, 32 are mirror-symmetric and 27 are chiral; count both hands of the chiral ones and you have 86 physically distinct solids. Only 18 of the 59 are fully supported.

The program does not enforce Miller's rules for you — it lets you build whatever you like, including the floating spikes above. What it gives you is the support relation, so that a supported solid is always one gesture away.

6 · Four famous solids, by hand

Now the payoff. Each of these is a well-known polyhedron, and each is a handful of clicks in the table.

The capital letters are Coxeter's mainline. A stellation that takes every cell out to some shell is named after that shell in capitals: A is the core, B is core plus shell b, and so on to H, everything. Those eight are the mainline stellations, and four of the six above are among them. Mixed selections concatenate — Ef₁ means "everything through shell e, plus the f₁ cells". That is the same information as {0,1,2,3,4,5(1)}, written by hand in 1938.

7 · The one that comes in two hands

Remember layer 5's second bundle, the one that split into halves marked 0 and 1. Take everything up to layer 4, then add just one of those halves:

The two hands:

Left and right are genuinely different solids: no rotation carries one onto the other, only a reflection. Take both halves and the result is mirror-symmetric once more.

This is the reason the program asks for two symmetry groups rather than one. Build under Ih and the halves are welded together and you can never have just one. Build under I and the choice opens up.

The textbook case is a pair of solids usually credited to Edmund Hess in 1876, though Cauchy seems to have known them in 1812. Five regular tetrahedra can be inscribed in a dodecahedron, and the resulting compound of five tetrahedra has only the 60 rotations — it is chiral, and there are two of them, mirror images that no rotation can interchange. Put both together and you get the compound of ten tetrahedra, which is mirror-symmetric again. Both are stellations of the icosahedron, and in cell language they differ in exactly one place. Both take every cell out to shell e. Then the ten-tetrahedron compound takes the whole chiral bundle in shell f, while each five-tetrahedron compound takes one of its two halves. Choosing a sub-cell instead of its parent is literally the difference between building one and building the other.

You have been looking at a famous solid. The selection in this figure, {0,1,2,3,4,5(1[1])}, is Du Val's Ef₁ — the compound of five tetrahedra, one of the five regular compounds, and one of only two chiral stellations of the icosahedron that are fully supported. Both counts agree: 1+20+30+60+20+60 cells out to shell e, plus 60 for one hand of f₁, is 251, which is the figure in the literature.

And it is what the original program shipped as an example. sample_02.stel, one of four .stel files in the repository, contains exactly this string. It is also precisely what a single shift-click on that one half-bundle produces, because its supporting set is everything below it.

8 · Your turn

Here is a table with nothing selected and no rails. Some things to try:

  • Click 0 in row 0 to put the icosahedron back.
  • Click a box far down the table, then shift-click the same box — watch the difference between a floating shell and a supported solid.
  • ⌥-click (or right-click) a cell in a low layer and see everything resting on it disappear with it.
  • Click a row number to take a whole layer.
  • Hover any box: the readout names the cell, how many congruent pieces it has, and its volume.

The string underneath is the same notation the program saves in its files. {0,1,2} means layers 0, 1 and 2 entire; 5(1[1]) means layer 5, bundle 1, half 1.

9 · Where this comes from

Almost nothing in the Cells table is new. The shells are Du Val's, the selection rules are Miller's, the diagram is Brückner's, and the solids are mostly Hess's and Poinsot's. What the program adds is that you can click them.

Why a table at all? Most stellation software asks you to pick cells geometrically — click a region of the stellation diagram, or a face of the solid. That is intuitive, but it answers "which cell is behind this facet?" only indirectly, and one click can cascade into many cells. A table instead shows you the whole state space at once, at the cost of abstraction.

Of the stellation programs written since 1980, a survey turns up essentially only Bulatov's that offers a layers × cells × sub-cells grid. The nearest relative is Great Stella's cell diagram, which holds the same information drawn as a graph rather than a grid — and its author cautions in his own manual that it is usually not an intuitive way to build stellations.

What makes the grid pay for itself here is the third column: the sub-cell split, which only appears once you let the stellation have less symmetry than the polyhedron. Take that away and the table really is just a list. With it, the table is the only place the handedness of a solid is something you can point at.
